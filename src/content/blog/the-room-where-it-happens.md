"Now, I am become Death. The destroyer of worlds."

The new off-broadway show Data inspires me in framing my future career role as an architect of the relationship among human, data, and society in the age of surveillance capitalism. 

Modern “machine intelligence” is reaching a tipping point of no return. I am not talking about AGI, although that could be interesting on its own -- but rather the mere increment of the predictive power of algorithms. Even without AGI, this predictive power of the new algorithms alone is enough to change the relationship of individuals with society and become the next nuclear weapon of the 21st century.

Data tells two engineers' moral struggle in becoming whistleblowers of their company's government contract in developing mass surveillance projects. The play reveals two central moral concerns -- one is in the data collection, another is in the extraction of information and decisions based on the data.

Today, both the collection and extraction of data have achieved economies of scale. Whatever the underlying reasons, we, as a mass of "users", have voluntarily given up our rights to privacy free for companies to exploit. A vicious cycle of data has been established (whether artificially or accidentally is another question) -- because of the user's personal data, they are able to predict the user's behaviour and provide better services, then in return ask for more data. The "gold dust" such as our keyboard inputs and behavioral patterns that are virtually free to us have proven to be extremely valuable to the companies in mass, which only recently became possible.

This new regime shift was observed in Susan Zoboff's Age of Surveillance Capitalism. User's "behavioural surplus," originally external to the market, were being captured and processed by the new “surveillance capitalists” (ex. Google, Meta...). They have created the "behavioural future market" in which commodifies the users and our behavioural surplus for their buyers such as the advertisers or national security agency.[^1] The users, as opposed to traditional consumers, have become a means to extract data rather than the end. However, the "property rights" to privacy is not well-defined and its market structure lacks study.[^2]

Then, who sets the rules of this new production relationship? How will the surveillance capitalist act? Who decides how they will act? Hence this is the position I want to put myself in -- the one with the most market power -- the architect of this new production relationship.

I have to be in the room where it happens.

For the first time, I feel a strong sense of conviction. And how closely connected is this calling to society and my interest/comparative advantage in data science! It is also narrow enough so my next steps are clear -- continue taking data science and economics classes and work my way to a PhD; but it is also broad enough in which there are multiple directions for me to consider -- economics, statistics, and even computer science. This is a problem I want to solve and not just an end point I want to achieve. 

In that sense, there are many possibilities ahead—but not so many as to feel directionless. With this clarity, I feel a new kind of confidence. I am invincible. Every choice leads me to something good. Nothing can go wrong.

This is where I am now.

And now, I have work to do.

[^1]: Of course, this regime shift can be very subtle. This can be exemplified by the recent group litigation against section 230. Modern social media companies hide behind section 230 so that they would not be sued for any user comments.

[^2]: Here, I am talking about privacy as a form of property rights as prescribed in the Coase Theorem. The Coase Theorem states that if property rights are well-defined (for example, by legal institutions) and the transaction cost of the market is zero, then the market will adjust the distribution of property rights to its social optimal. What's enlightening about the Coase Theorem, however, lies in its assumption of zero transaction cost. In real life, the transaction cost is never zero. Therefore, it becomes instructive to study the market structure that increases the transaction cost and how these structures distort the distribution of property rights. What I am worried about specifically here is the inherent asymmetry of the market structure of the behavioral future market -- how many seconds we stay on a web page is virtually useless to any individual users. It's a winner-takes-all game even for the producers -- it is useless to only have a few hundred people's data or a few millions but you only know, say, when they sleep. Economics advises us that the monopolistic nature of the market leads to market failure -- but exactly what kind of market failure is a question leaves me pondering.
