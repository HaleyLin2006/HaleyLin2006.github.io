Feynman has his twelve favorite questions that he keeps in his mind. Right now, I don't really have twelve questions that I think about, but here's my attempt to list some of them.

## Big Questions

1. **New Modality of Economics Data**: The advent of modern AI has unlocked unprecedented predictive power that can be tamed to estimate causal effects. [Farrell, Liang, and Misra (2021)](https://doi.org/10.3982/ECTA16901) establish non-asymptotic high-probability bounds for deep neural nets that are fast enough to support valid semiparametric inference on causal parameters. [Imai and Nakamura (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2530532123) and subsequent work then use the internal representations of deep generative models to establish identification conditions and estimate the causal effects of high-dimensional treatments such as text, images, and video.

    What new research questions does this open up, now that treatments can be high-dimensional? And how can machine learning literatures such as causal representation learning and explainability inform causal inference in economics? My dream is to build an end-to-end pipeline that (i) automates the discovery of candidate causal mechanisms from observational data and (ii) estimates causal effects of potentially high-dimensional treatments under easy-to-check identification conditions that are applicable in various settings.
      
2. **Explainable AI for Science**: A principled, end-to-end explanation of why neural networks succeed remains elusive. However, many by-products of research on explainable AI may be immediately useful for scientific discovery. For example, [Radhakrishnan et al. (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2509860122) show that neural networks trained by gradient descent implicitly compute the Hilbert–Schmidt Independence Criterion (HSIC), a kernel-based generalization of covariance. Building on this, they develop a scalable measure of dependence that can detect relationships among tens of thousands of variables across millions of samples.

    How can we explain the immense predictive gains of modern AI in terms of the data and the architecture? How can understanding modern AI systems inform science, and vice versa? I am interested in developing explainable AI techniques within specific scientific settings, and think about how applications in these settings inform us how neural networks work.

4. **Sutton's Bitter Lesson**: Sutton's famous [blog post](http://www.incompleteideas.net/IncIdeas/BitterLesson.html) states that "The biggest lesson that can be read from 70 years of AI research is that general methods that leverage computation are ultimately the most effective, and by a large margin." To what extent does this idea extrapolate to other fields? What is scalable in economics? How can we leverage compute in economics? And what are the restrictions to this "bitter-lesson pilled" mindset?
