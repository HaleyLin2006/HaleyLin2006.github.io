I used to do a lot of Model UN in highschool (see list below). Here's an archive of background guides I was involved in, in order of how proud I am looking back. 

Taiwanese MUN is very different from the US. I still remember the time where we read hundreds of pages of UN speech records and rules of procedure in hope to make the conference as realistic as possible. I'm proud that all of these are done before the age of AI.

I am extremely grateful to all the colleagues I've had the privilege to work with: Sam Chen, Melvin Huang, Cayenne Wan, Christopher Wang, Andy Lin, Vanessa Yu, Michael Zhuang, Eric Chang, Iris Liu, Aisa Huang, Isa Chou, Jerry Chen, Andrew Chen, and Alex Kao. You all have such characters. You are the reason why I have so much fun in MUN and why I am here today.

1. **UNSC, Maintenance of International Peace and Security (focus on climate change), TCMUN 2024, with Andy Lin and Eric Chang**

    - [Background Guide](/MUN/TCMUN%20SC_Background%20Guide.pdf)
    - [Committee Introduction](/MUN/TCMUN%20SC_Committee%20Introduction.pdf) 
    - [Familiarizing Newly Elected Members (RoP)](/MUN/TCMUN%20SC_Familiarizing%20newly%20elected%20members%20with%20the%20work%20of%20the%20Council%20and%20its%20subsidiary%20bodies.pdf)

    **AI Summary**

    The committee asks whether, and how, the Security Council should treat climate change as a threat to international peace and security. The Council has never adopted a thematic resolution on climate and security. The 2021 draft (S/2021/990) had 113 co-sponsors but was vetoed by Russia, with India voting against and China abstaining.
    <details class="ai-summary">
    <summary>Details</summary>

    - *The issue:* Climate change works as a "threat multiplier." Roughly 70% of conflict-affected states are also among the most climate-vulnerable, and six of the ten largest UN peace operations sit in highly exposed countries. The Council has slipped climate language into country-specific texts (Lake Chad in Res. 2349, South Sudan in Res. 2625), but every mention is a negotiation. In the UNAMA mandate (Res. 2626), "climate change" was replaced with "drought", and S/PRST/2022/6 only passed after "security" became "stability."
    - *Major parties:* Climate-security advocates include the Group of Friends of Climate and Security (founded by Germany and Nauru, now 66 members), the Pacific Island states (who call for a Special Representative on climate and security) and the African Union (which pushes a climate–security–development nexus and early warning). The skeptics are Russia, China, India and Brazil. They argue climate is a sustainable-development issue that belongs in the GA, ECOSOC and UNFCCC, and that it distracts from the political roots of conflict. Russia calls it a "ticking time bomb" that would let the Council put any country on its agenda. Developing countries add the unmet USD 100 billion climate-finance pledge.
    - *The central argument:* The fight is over the Council's mandate: how broadly should "threat to the peace" be read? One side wants to institutionalize climate security through reporting, early warning, the Climate Security Mechanism and a special envoy. The other sees mandate creep and a threat to sovereignty. Much of the battle is fought over wording.

    </details>

3. **ECOSOC, Economic and social repercussions of the Israeli occupation on the living conditions of the Palestinian people in the Occupied Palestinian Territory, including East Jerusalem, and the Arab population in the occupied Syrian Golan.**
    **CentMUN 2023, with Vanessa Yu**

    - [Background Guide](/MUN/CentMUN%202023%20ECOSOC%20Background%20Guide.pdf)

    **AI Summary**

    This is ECOSOC's annual agenda item on the economic and social cost of the occupation. The guide was written in late 2023, in the shadow of the October 7 attack and the war in Gaza.
    <details class="ai-summary">
    <summary>Details</summary>

    - *The issue:* The guide frames the problem as two movements. "Pushing Palestinians Out" covers home demolitions, restricted access to water and land, and the fragmentation of territory through movement restrictions. "Welcoming Israelis In" covers settlement expansion. Past efforts include UNSC resolutions, the 2003 Quartet Roadmap toward a two-state solution and the most recent ECOSOC Resolution 2023/34.
    - *Major parties:* The United States backs Israel's right to self-defense under Article 51 and votes against the ECOSOC resolution as "unbalanced" and politicized. Palestine points to the humanitarian collapse in Gaza and decades of displacement. Israel justifies its control of the West Bank, Gaza and the Golan on security grounds. Saudi Arabia was on the "cusp" of normalizing relations with Israel under US mediation, a process now strained by the war, so its statements are carefully balanced. Iran is openly combative and calls for punitive action against Israel.
    - *The central argument:* Do the Geneva Conventions and the two human-rights Covenants apply in the occupied territories, and can "military necessity" justify Israel's measures? The other question is what ECOSOC, unlike the GA or Security Council, can actually say. Because ECOSOC resolutions run on long preambles, much of the debate will be over which facts to report and which political statements to make.

    </details>

4. **UNSC, Situation of Afghanistan, MDMUN 2023, with Christopher Wang and Sam Chen**

    - [Background Guide](/MUN/2023%20MDMUN%20UNSC%20BGG.pdf)

    **AI Summary**

    The committee deals with Afghanistan after the Taliban takeover of August 2021. The country is run by de facto authorities that almost no one recognizes and that remain under UN sanctions.
    <details class="ai-summary">
    <summary>Details</summary>

    - *The issue:* The guide splits the problem into four parts. On human rights, girls are barred from school past sixth grade, women are banned from most work (including for NGOs and the UN), press freedom has collapsed and there is no codified rule of law. On food security, about two-thirds of the population relies on aid and 20 million people face food insecurity. On sanctions and the economy, the 1267/1988 sanctions on the Taliban still apply now that they are the government, about USD 9 billion in central-bank reserves is frozen, and banks avoid Afghanistan, which together produce a liquidity crisis. The fourth part is security and terrorism.
    - *Major parties:* The Taliban want recognition and an end to foreign interference. The United States is the largest donor but prioritizes counter-terrorism, holds about USD 7 billion in Afghan assets and is reluctant to engage. Russia offers regional diplomacy and counter-terrorism cooperation without military involvement. China wants to extend the Belt and Road Initiative and CPEC into Afghanistan, calls the Taliban the "interim government", blames the US and demands that the assets be released. Pakistan's relationship with the Taliban has soured over Pakistani Taliban (TTP) attacks, which led to Pakistan's 2022 airstrikes.
    - *The central argument:* The guide says outright that sanctions are the underlying theme: does your country want a "stable Afghanistan" or a "legitimate Afghanistan"? One side wants to engage with the Taliban and unblock aid and assets to stop the humanitarian collapse. The other wants to keep up the pressure on human rights and women's rights, because easing sanctions looks like recognizing the Taliban.

    </details>

5. **UNSC, 2025 Review of the UN Peacebuilding Architecture, TransMUN 2024, with Iris Liu and Melvin Huang**

    - [Background Guide](/MUN/Background%20Guide%20of%20UNSC.pdf)

    **AI Summary**

    The committee previews the 2025 review of the UN Peacebuilding Architecture. It was created in 2005 (SC Res. 1645 / GA Res. 60/180) and has three parts: the Peacebuilding Commission (PBC), the Peacebuilding Fund (PBF) and the Peacebuilding Support Office (PBSO).
    <details class="ai-summary">
    <summary>Details</summary>

    - *The issue:* The guide raises three problems. The first is the scope of peacebuilding. Case studies from South Sudan and the Central African Republic show how a narrow focus on ceasefires, rushed elections and budget-driven early withdrawals lead conflicts to relapse. The second is peacebuilding's place in the UN. The GA sees development and human rights as outside the Security Council's remit, the Council suspects peacebuilding is a "backdoor" for the GA into security, and the PBC's relationship with the Council is ad hoc. The third is inadequate, unpredictable funding.
    - *Major parties:* The Non-Aligned Movement stresses sovereignty and non-interference and supplies many of the troops and police for UN missions. China takes a "developmental peace" approach and insists on state consent. Russia puts security first, stresses sovereignty and uses its veto. The United States sees state fragility as the root cause and focuses on building democratic institutions. France supports Council reform and restraint on the veto, and wants more investment in the PBC and PBF. Germany is the largest PBF donor and calls for adequate financing. The African Union frames peacebuilding as addressing the colonial roots of conflict.
    - *The central argument:* How far can broad, multidimensional peacebuilding go without violating sovereignty and national ownership? The other questions are how to divide the work among the GA, the Security Council and the PBC, how to make the PBC–Council relationship regular and predictable, and how to put the Peacebuilding Fund on a sustainable footing.

    </details>

6. **UNGA, The Right to Development, PMUN 2025, with Michael Zhuang**

    - [Background Guide](/MUN/Phoenix%20MUN%20X%20General%20Assembly%20Background%20Guide.pdf)

    **AI Summary**

    The General Assembly debates what the right to development actually means in practice. The 1986 Declaration on the Right to Development (Res. 41/128) passed 146–1, with the United States the lone vote against and 8 abstentions. The guide warns that the debate will be political and position-based, not solution-based, and will split roughly along developed and developing lines.
    <details class="ai-summary">
    <summary>Details</summary>

    - *The issue:* The right to development grew out of the Cold War split between the two Covenants (ICCPR and ICESCR), Kéba M'Baye's 1972 argument that the North owes the South compensation for colonial-era trade advantages, and the failed New International Economic Order (NIEO), which never got past GA resolutions. There are three subtopics. *Development financing* covers the Secretary-General's call for a USD 500 billion annual SDG stimulus and an SDG financing gap of about USD 4.2 trillion a year. *Debt* covers 18 sovereign defaults across 10 developing countries since COVID, a record USD 443.5 billion in external debt payments in 2022, and the non-binding Guiding Principles on Foreign Debt and Human Rights. *Representation* covers the IMF and World Bank, where 68 low- and middle-income countries with 21.7% of the world's population hold only 5.3% of IMF voting power.
    - *Major parties:* Most developed countries, especially in the Western European and Others Group, vote against or abstain on right-to-development resolutions. They want it to stay "soft law" and fear a "right to everything" that obliges them to pay. Moderates such as Germany support the idea but abstain, so that repeated yes votes don't harden it into customary law. The United States will discuss an individual's right to development but not a state's, because nations don't hold human rights. On the other side, the Non-Aligned Movement and the African Union (backed by ASEAN and CARICOM) treat it as a collective right of peoples, rooted in the 1981 Banjul Charter, South-South cooperation and the Monterrey Consensus. A NAM draft at the 57th Human Rights Council stressing realization "at the international level" was rejected.
    - *The central argument:* Is the right to development "national" or "international"? Developing countries read it as a duty of international cooperation: aid, debt relief, technology transfer and reform of the IMF and World Bank. Developed countries point to Article 3 (states bear primary responsibility for their own development) and Article 2 (the human person, not the state, is the beneficiary). Delegates have to argue from treaties and legal instruments for the legal basis, or lack of one, for claims that rich countries must pay.

    </details>

7. **中華民國立法院 交通委員會, 人工智慧基本法, 模擬立法院 2024, with Aisa Huang and Isa Chou**

    - [Background Guide (背景文件)](/MUN/MLY%202024%20TransCon_%E8%83%8C%E6%99%AF%E6%96%87%E4%BB%B6%E7%B5%82%E7%A8%BF.pdf)

    **AI Summary**

    本委員會為立法院交通委員會（主管數位發展事務），審查《人工智慧基本法》。背景文件比較三份草案：中國國民黨吳宗憲委員等人之提案、台灣民眾黨黨團之《人工智慧發展及管理條例》，以及行政院國家科學及技術委員會（國科會）之草案。軍事用途之人工智慧不在本委員會職權範圍內。
    <details class="ai-summary">
    <summary>Details</summary>

    - *議題：* 三份草案對人工智慧的定義與基本原則大致相同，分歧在於監管的方式與時機。隱私方面，民眾黨版增加去識別化義務，並參照 GDPR 限制敏感資料的使用；風險分級方面，民眾黨版明定歐盟式四級制度（不可接受風險、高風險、特定風險、風險極小），國科會版則交由數位發展部訂定分級框架；安全監測計畫與公開技術文件引發營業秘密的疑慮；標示、紀錄功能與罰則則僅見於民眾黨版。產業促進方面，三版皆設有「創新實驗環境」（監理沙盒），民眾黨版與國科會版另提出供新創使用的巨量資料平台。
    - *各方立場：* 國民黨版強調扶植中小企業，對問責著墨較少；民眾黨版參照歐盟，條文最為詳細具體；行政院（國科會）版則偏好保留彈性的框架，細節留待後續標準與法規訂定。
    - *核心爭點：* 政府如何平衡產業發展與監管？作為「基本法」，應規範到多細，又該保留多少彈性？背景文件指出，問責將是最大爭點：民眾黨版採「推定過失」與「推定因果關係」（參照歐盟《人工智慧責任指令草案》），國科會版留待未來標準訂定，國民黨版則無相關條文。若自駕車發生事故，舉證責任該由誰承擔？

    </details>

8. **UNSC, Questions Concerning Haiti, MDMUN 2024, with Andy Lin and Christopher Wang**

    - [Background Guide](/MUN/MDMUN%2014th%20UNSC%20BGG.pdf)

    **AI Summary**

    The committee deals with Haiti's collapse into gang rule after Prime Minister Ariel Henry left the country and gangs, including the G-9 coalition, seized most of Port-au-Prince.
    <details class="ai-summary">
    <summary>Details</summary>

    - *The issue:* Gangs control roads, ports and government buildings. In January and February 2024 alone, 1,436 civilians were killed, injured or kidnapped. Gangs commit widespread sexual violence and recruit children, and vigilante "self-defense brigades" carry out lynchings. The Haitian National Police is outgunned and understaffed: more than 1,600 officers left in 2023. Political progress has stalled, and illicit weapons keep flowing in. Past responses include targeted sanctions (Res. 2653), a string of missions (UNMIH, MINUSTAH and its cholera legacy, MINUJUSTH, BINUH) and the Kenya-led Multinational Security Support Mission authorized in 2023 (Res. 2699).
    - *Major parties:* Russia and China abstained on the security-support mission. They cited a hasty process, unclear objectives and the gravity of using Chapter VII to authorize a non-UN force, and they stress sovereignty. The EU funds human-rights work and BINUH and applies its own sanctions on gang leaders. The United States is Haiti's largest donor, but its history of intervention makes it a contested actor. CARICOM pushes for Haitian-led solutions and an inclusive political transition.
    - *The central argument:* The guide names it directly: how can the Security Council act quickly against non-state armed gangs without intruding on Haiti's sovereignty? Effective help on policing, security and governance pulls one way. The lessons of past missions and the principle of non-interference pull the other.

    </details>

9. **US Congress, DOGE Sub-Committee, TransMUN 2025, DOGE Sub-Committee, with Jerry Chen and Sam Chen**

    - [Background Guide](/MUN/TransMUN%202025%20DOGE%20Background%20Guide.pdf)

    **AI Summary**

    Delegates play members of the House Oversight Committee's "Delivering on Government Efficiency" (DOGE) Subcommittee. The timeline starts on January 20, 2025, and the goal is to pass, or block, a budget-cutting bill. The guide says outright that the committee is partisan and that narrative matters more than substance.
    <details class="ai-summary">
    <summary>Details</summary>

    - *The issue:* There are three hearings. "Anti-American Airwaves" targets the more than USD 500 million a year in federal funding for public broadcasting, mainly NPR and PBS. "America Last" targets USAID and foreign aid. "Unfair Play" covers transgender athletes in women's sports under Title IX. A legal primer covers the tools for making cuts (appropriations, rescissions, reconciliation), the Administrative Procedure Act and the unitary-executive debate (*Humphrey's Executor*).
    - *Major parties:* Republicans call NPR and PBS left-wing "echo chambers", call USAID an "America-last slush fund" and want to protect women's sports. Democrats defend public broadcasting as trusted, local and educational, mock the hearings (Elmo and the Communist Party), and say the majority is avoiding real oversight such as Signalgate. Witnesses such as the PBS CEO, along with advocacy groups on both sides of the sports debate, shape the record.
    - *The central argument:* Are these programs wasteful, "un-American" spending, or is this a culture war presented as fiscal discipline? A second, institutional question sits underneath: who really controls executive agencies, Congress or the President? And can any cuts be written so they survive in court?

    </details>

10. **ECOSOC, Economic and Political Rights of Minority Groups, AceMUN 2023, with Andrew Chen and Alex Kao**

    - [Background Guide](/MUN/AceMUN%202023%20ECOSOC%20Background%20Guide.pdf)

    **AI Summary**

    ECOSOC takes up the rights of minority groups through two separate agendas, each with its own draft resolution: women's rights, framed as a test of whether human rights are universal, and the political participation of Indigenous peoples in the UN. 
    <details class="ai-summary">
    <summary>Details</summary>

    - *The issue:* On women's rights, the guide asks whether human rights are a universal standard or a "Western" concept, using mainstream interpretations of Islamic law as its main (explicitly non-judgmental) example. It covers women's underrepresentation in politics (only six countries have 50% or more women in parliament) and practices such as child marriage (about 640 million girls) and female genital mutilation (at least 200 million women and girls). On Indigenous peoples, the 2007 UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples (UNDRIP) promises participation in decisions that affect them but isn't binding. Indigenous voices are scattered across UN bodies, and the ECOSOC rules for NGO consultative status (Res. 1996/31) don't fit Indigenous groups. Those rules require broad international representation, a headquarters and two years of existence, and some Indigenous bodies are self-governing or hereditary.
    - *Major parties:* The guide has no country-by-country section. On women's rights, it sets universalists, who read the UDHR and CEDAW as binding on every culture, against states that defend religious and cultural practice as their own legitimate framework. On Indigenous participation, the key bodies are ECOSOC's Permanent Forum on Indigenous Issues (8 state nominees and 8 Indigenous members) and the Human Rights Council's Expert Mechanism. States are wary of giving groups such as the Sami Parliament, or the Dalai Lama as the guide's own example, a status they haven't approved.
    - *The central argument:* How far should cultural and religious freedom limit the basic rights of women? For Indigenous peoples, the question is how much power their representatives should have: speaking only, or drafting and voting too? Should that happen in every UN meeting or only in dedicated forums? And who decides which groups qualify without violating their right to self-identification? More representation could also slow the committee down and make consensus harder.

    </details>
